# Research Background

This document explains the reasoning behind two of RAGeval's core design choices — using an LLM as
the groundedness/faithfulness judge, and using a panel of judges rather than one — and situates the
project relative to the broader RAG-evaluation research landscape. For the empirical validation of
these choices (actual numbers from a real benchmark run), see
[`eval/JUDGE_BENCHMARK.md`](eval/JUDGE_BENCHMARK.md). This document is the "why"; that one is the
"what we measured."

RAGeval is a practical, applied tool. It draws on established ideas from the LLM-evaluation
literature and applies them to production RAG observability — it does not claim to introduce novel
evaluation methodology of its own.

## Why an LLM judge, not just embedding similarity

A common, cheap way to approximate "is this answer grounded in the retrieved context?" is to
compute embedding similarity between the answer and the context chunks. That signal is useful and
RAGeval uses it too (for retrieval relevance and as a faithfulness proxy), but it has a well-known
blind spot: embedding similarity measures semantic/topical closeness, not logical consistency. An
answer can be embedded very close to its source context while still contradicting it, inverting a
number, dropping a critical qualifier, or asserting something the context never actually said.
Conversely, a well-grounded answer that paraphrases heavily or uses different vocabulary than the
source can score lower on pure similarity than its actual faithfulness would warrant. This
gap — similarity captures "about the same topic," not "actually entailed by the source" — is a
recurring, general observation across the RAG-evaluation literature, and it's the basic motivation
for adding a reasoning-capable judge (an LLM that can read the context and the answer together and
assess entailment) rather than relying on a single similarity score alone.

## Why multiple judges, not one

Using an LLM to grade another LLM's output ("LLM-as-a-judge") is itself a well-established
technique, but a single judge model brings its own documented biases into the evaluation:

- **Verbosity bias** — a tendency to rate longer, more elaborated answers as better independent of
  actual correctness.
- **Self-preference bias** — a judge model favoring outputs that resemble its own style or that
  were generated by a similar model family.
- **Position bias** — in pairwise comparison setups, a tendency to favor whichever answer is
  presented first (or second).

These are widely discussed failure modes in the 2023–2026 LLM-evaluation literature. One recognized
mitigation is to use a panel of several judge models — often smaller or cheaper ones — and combine
their votes, rather than trusting a single large judge's opinion. This "jury" or "panel of LLM
evaluators" line of work (see the same panel-of-judges prior art referenced in
[`eval/JUDGE_BENCHMARK.md`](eval/JUDGE_BENCHMARK.md#2026-landscape)) is the direct inspiration for
RAGeval's design: no single-judge fallback, at least two independently configured judge models
scoring every query, and the consensus (plus the judges' disagreement/stdev) surfaced rather than
collapsed into an opaque single number. The idea is that biases specific to one model are less
likely to be shared across judges from different providers and model families, so averaging (or
requiring agreement) damps out at least some of that noise. This is a plausibility argument backed
by general literature, not a claim that RAGeval's specific two- or three-judge setup has been
independently validated at scale — see the benchmark doc's caveats for what has actually been
measured.

## Where RAGeval sits in the RAG-evaluation landscape

RAGeval is one of several tools built specifically to evaluate RAG pipelines rather than general
LLM outputs, and its design overlaps with — while differing in emphasis from — a few established
frameworks. RAGAS evaluates RAG systems along a triad of metrics (answer relevance, faithfulness,
and context precision/recall), computed largely via LLM-based scoring against the retrieved
context and reference answers. ARES takes a somewhat different approach, using classifier models
fine-tuned (often with synthetic training data) to predict relevance/faithfulness judgments,
positioning itself as a lighter-weight alternative to always calling a large LLM for every
judgment. TruLens frames its evaluation around a "RAG triad" — context relevance, groundedness, and
answer relevance — computed with configurable feedback functions, including LLM-based ones. RAGeval
overlaps most closely with the groundedness/faithfulness piece of that landscape, but narrows its
focus to production observability (drop-in instrumentation, cost and latency tracking, a
self-hosted dashboard) and differentiates its scoring approach specifically through multi-judge
consensus rather than a single configurable judge. It does not attempt to replicate RAGAS's or
ARES's full metric suites, and reasonable users evaluating a RAG pipeline in 2026 might use RAGeval
for its observability/consensus-groundedness angle alongside — not necessarily instead of — one of
these other frameworks for other axes of evaluation.
